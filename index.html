About

My work combines electron microscopy with the synthesis of carbon and 2D materials. I did my PhD in Japan, spent four years at the Cambridge Graphene Centre on the EU Graphene Flagship, and came to Bristol in 2024.

Managing Bristol's electron microscopy suite brings me into contact with a wide range of materials and research questions. Alongside facility research and training, I develop projects on carbon, 2D materials and sodium solid-state electrolytes with colleagues in the School of Chemistry.

I am always glad to hear from people with a problem that needs watching rather than only measuring.

Research

Understanding how materials form and change.

I synthesise materials and study how they respond to heat, electrical bias and contact with other materials.

Current

Batteries and energy materials

My current work explores coatings for sodium solid-state electrolytes, combining sol-gel synthesis, computational screening and microscopy to investigate interfacial stability. Related earlier collaborations include work on lithium-carbon nanocomposites for safer handling of lithium metal, and on catalyst sites for zinc-air batteries using virtual screening and machine learning.

Current

In situ and operando microscopy

Heating and biasing holders let me follow catalytic graphitisation, electromigration and the formation of carbon nanostructures while they are happening. The intermediate states are usually where the answer is, and they are exactly what you lose if you only look at the finished sample.

Metal catalysed carbon growth

Catalyst choice strongly influences carbon growth and structure. My work has examined ten catalyst systems, from Fe, Ni and Cu to Mo, Sn, In, Ga, Co, Pd and CuNi alloy. That work produced graphene growth at 180 °C on tin and 150 °C on indium.

Wafer scale 2D materials

At the Cambridge Graphene Centre I worked on CVD growth of graphene, hBN, MoS2 and WS2 for the EU Graphene Flagship, and demonstrated wafer-scale growth up to 200 mm. Growing the material is rarely the hard part. Growing it the same everywhere, then lifting it off and onto something useful without damaging it, is.

Carbon from waste

Waste and renewable feedstocks can provide carbon sources for graphene synthesis. I have made it from solid waste plastic, and worked with others on waste chicken fat and other renewable precursors. The question is always the same one: what do the precursor and the catalyst do to the carbon you end up with?

Methods

How the work gets done.

Methods I use across research and facility work.

  • Electron microscopy

    TEM and STEM: HRTEM, HAADF, EELS, EDX, SAED, CBED and two beam diffraction contrast imaging. In situ and operando work with heating and biasing holders. SEM and EDX. Specimen preparation for powders, films and cross sections.

  • Growth & deposition

    CVD at atmospheric and low pressure, MOCVD, magnetron sputtering and thermal evaporation, up to 200 mm wafers. Sol-gel synthesis. Polymer assisted transfer of 2D films onto other substrates.

  • Supporting characterisation

    Raman spectroscopy, XPS, AFM and XRD, used to tie processing conditions back to structure and properties.

  • Computation

    Python for diffraction and imaging data, tools of my own for SAED and XRD pattern analysis, and generative model screening of candidate materials on HPC before anything is committed to the lab.

  • Instruments

    I run the electron microscope suite in the Chemical Imaging Facility, a JEOL 2100F FEG TEM and a JEOL 1400, and take on analytical work and method development for groups inside and outside the university.
